# Is this year's picture the whole picture?

You've left your first budget briefing knowing what "balanced" really means. The trajectory clock runs underneath the annual numbers, and three questions reveal what it's telling you: two that look backward, at where you are and how you got there, and one that looks forward, toward the steward's greatest responsibility.

---

## How do our assets compare to our liabilities?

### Why it's harder than it looks

Your government produces [GLOSSARY:annual_financial_statements]annual financial statements[/GLOSSARY], which can tell you a lot about the change in your position over time. In fact, it contains something called a [GLOSSARY:statement_of_net_position]statement of net position[/GLOSSARY]. This is [GLOSSARY:assets]assets[/GLOSSARY] minus [GLOSSARY:liabilities]liabilities[/GLOSSARY] and it is one of the most important signals available to you. When [GLOSSARY:net_position]net position[/GLOSSARY] is growing, that is genuinely good news. When it is shrinking, that is a signal worth taking seriously.

[L1]

### What every steward needs to know

Ask whether your government's overall [GLOSSARY:net_position]net position[/GLOSSARY] — assets minus liabilities — is getting stronger or weaker over time, and look at the trend across several years rather than a single snapshot.

Treat net position as one signal among several, not the whole picture. Like all signals, it has limits. A good steward knows how to look beyond the numbers.

[/L1]

[BILLBOARD]Many assets and liabilities will have impacts long into the future. Understand which ones will have big impact in the next few years.[/BILLBOARD]
[EXIT]Here's what to ask →[/EXIT]

[L2]

- Do we have an asset inventory that tracks the condition of major [GLOSSARY:capital_assets]assets[/GLOSSARY] over time?
- For any new capital asset in the pipeline, do we know what it will cost to operate and where that money will come from?
- Is our [GLOSSARY:postemployment_benefit_obligations]pension liability[/GLOSSARY] shrinking or growing this year, and are the assumptions behind it realistic and current?

[/L2]

## Are we following our own reserve policies?

### Why it's harder than it looks

[GLOSSARY:reserves]Reserves[/GLOSSARY] serve two distinct purposes. First is insurance — a cushion against unplanned, unavoidable costs like a natural disaster, a recession, or a sudden revenue shortfall. Second is savings, accumulated funds for projects that wouldn't be affordable in a single year. The discipline is keeping these two purposes separate, and avoiding using either for [GLOSSARY:ongoing_expenses]ongoing expenses[/GLOSSARY]. Using reserves to balance the [GLOSSARY:budget]budget[/GLOSSARY] is the financial equivalent of eating your seed corn — it solves a short-term problem by making the next one worse. Reserves are also an early warning signal: a government quietly drifting in the wrong direction will often start drawing down reserves.

[L1]

### What every steward needs to know

Ask whether your government has a formal reserve policy, and whether it defines a goldilocks zone for reserves — not too little, not too much. Then compare actual reserve levels against that policy range and ask what accounts for any gap.

Also ask whether reserves are being used to cover ongoing [GLOSSARY:operating_costs]operating costs[/GLOSSARY]. If they are, that's a warning sign. If reserves were drawn down for a legitimate reason — like a disaster response or a planned capital purchase — ask whether there's a policy to rebuild them, and whether it's being followed.

[/L1]

[BILLBOARD]How much reserves are enough? It depends — and the answer is specific to your community.[/BILLBOARD]
[EXIT]Here's what to ask →[/EXIT]

[L2]

- Have we carefully analyzed our risks to set the reserve level?
- How have our reserve levels trended over the past several years — steadily healthy, or quietly eroding?
- When reserves have been drawn down for a legitimate purpose, do we follow a policy to rebuild them?

[/L2]

## Where are we headed?

### Why it's harder than it looks

Many governments do not have a [GLOSSARY:long_term_financial_forecast]long-term financial forecast[/GLOSSARY]. Those that do often project past trends forward without accounting for changes in the environment that could alter those trends. The annual [GLOSSARY:budget]budget[/GLOSSARY] focuses on the short term, while the forces that determine long-term trajectory — demographic shifts, economic changes, aging infrastructure, [GLOSSARY:compounding]compounding obligations[/GLOSSARY] — operate on a much longer timescale and are easy to ignore when the annual numbers look fine. A forecast is not a perfect prediction; it's a tool for surfacing assumptions, identifying risks, and making the consequences of current decisions visible before they become irreversible.

[L1]

### What every steward needs to know

Ask whether your government has a [GLOSSARY:long_term_financial_forecast]long-term financial forecast[/GLOSSARY], and how many years out it looks.

Then ask whether that forecast only extends past trends forward, or accounts for changes like demographic shifts, economic changes, and aging infrastructure. A forecast that extends the past is good, but one that addresses a changing world is better. Finally, ask what the forecast says about the sustainability of your government's current trajectory.

[/L1]

[BILLBOARD]A forecast tells you where you're headed and it should tell you what could knock you off course.[/BILLBOARD]
[EXIT]Here's what to ask →[/EXIT]

[L2]

- What are the biggest risks to our long-term financial health, and are they reflected in our forecast?
- What decisions are we making today that will be hardest to reverse later?

[/L2]
